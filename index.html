Breakup Triage

A self-guided 35-day protocol for the month after a breakup: seven days of acute triage, then 28 days of structured rebuilding. This page documents what the course is, how a day works, what it draws on, and how it is designed.

01What it is

In one paragraph

Breakup Triage is a fixed, self-directed sequence of 35 daily lessons. Each day gives you one short reading, one audio track of twelve to twenty minutes, and one concrete task you can finish that day. You work through it alone, at your own pace. There are no calls, no community, and no one watching.

It is a structured self-guided protocol, not therapy and not coaching. The sequence is fixed: seven days of acute triage for the immediate aftermath, then 28 days of structured rebuilding. Every day follows the same shape — a mechanism, some measures, and a task — so the course never asks you to decide what to do next.

It doesn’t diagnose or treat mental health conditions, and it can’t promise a reunion. What it offers is a little structure and practical support while you move through something that has its own timeline.

02How a day works

Each lesson opens with a short guided physical reset, then explains exactly one mechanism, then hands you one task. The task always produces something that survives the day: a checklist, a log, a card, a page you keep.

Listen or read
Twelve to twenty minutes of audio, with the full text alongside it. Most people read instead of listening at night.
The task
Five to twenty minutes, finishable the same day. No task requires another person.
What you keep
The artifacts become the record you review on Day 7, Day 28 and Day 35 — the evidence that something changed.
Pace
One lesson a day, or whenever you get to it. Nothing expires, there are no streaks, and there are no days to make up.
The end of every lesson
Clinical boundaries and crisis resources. Every lesson, not just the first one.

Five stages, each with one job. The stage functions are deliberate: the acute week is not for explaining your relationship, and after Day 14 your ex stops being an object of analysis.

04What it draws on

Each lesson teaches one mechanism, and names where it comes from when that matters. The course uses a small set of established ideas and stays away from the ones it cannot support.

  • Withdrawal, not weakness. The first week treats the longing as a physical process with a shape and a timetable, which is why the early lessons are about contact containment and the body rather than insight.
  • Grief. Grief is referenced across the course, and the course never claims to shorten it. You are not asked to stop feeling it; you are given something to do alongside it.
  • Limerence. Day 12 introduces Dorothy Tennov’s 1979 research on limerence, with the explicit caveat that it is a common pattern rather than a diagnosis.
  • Memory as reconstruction. Days 4 and 12 treat recall as something that gets smoother each time you replay it, which is why both days work with written comparison instead of argument.
  • Rumination and attention. Days 17 and 20 work on where attention goes and on hearing a thought as a sentence rather than a verdict.
  • Self-efficacy. Days 15 to 21 deliberately build evidence rather than self-esteem talk: confidence is treated as something assembled from completed actions.
  • Habits, sleep and the body. Days 22 to 28 are about the first hour, the ledger, the blocks and the energy map — the practical ground under everything else.
  • Boundaries and closure. Contact containment is graded rather than all-or-nothing, and Day 14 exists so that the relationship can be filed away instead of re-litigated forever.

Two things are kept out on purpose: neuroscience vocabulary used as decoration, and any language that treats pain as a gift.

05How it is designed

These are the rules the course was written to, not marketing decisions. They are the reason it looks the way it does.

  1. One mechanism per lesson. No lesson introduces a second school of thought. If two ideas conflict, the lesson says which one it is using and why.
  2. Claims carry their own confidence. Established findings, popular claims and folk advice are marked differently, and phrased as “many people find” or “some research suggests” when that is all that can honestly be said.
  3. Every lesson ends in an artifact. A task you finish the same day and keep. From Day 15 onward this is a hard requirement, because the course is building evidence, not momentum.
  4. The acute week refuses to explain things. Days 1 to 7 forbid root-cause analysis and life planning. Explaining your relationship in week one is how people stay in it.
  5. Day 14 closes the file. After it, your ex stops being an object of analysis. The file is sealed for three months, not destroyed.
  6. Nothing depends on a coach. No calls, no forums, no accountability partner. The structure has to work when nobody is watching.
  7. Safety rails on physical tasks. Walks get daylight routes; lifting and ladders get limits. Physical instructions are written to be safe for someone sleeping badly and eating little.
  8. Honesty about the ceiling. The 28 days are meant to restore function — sleep, appetite, attention, routine — not to guarantee that anything is resolved.

06What it is not

It is
A fixed sequence you follow · 7 + 28 days, one task a day · something you can use at 2 a.m. · written in plain, honest language
It is not
A manipulation guide to beg or scheme them back · twelve weeks of inspiration · a magical cure · relationship gossip
It is not clinical care
No diagnosis, no treatment, no substitute for a therapist or a doctor. If you are already working with one, this sits alongside that work rather than replacing it.
It makes no promises
Not about getting back together, not about stopping the pain on schedule, and not about “closing the chapter” by Day 35.

07Why I built it

I’m an independent developer, not a therapist or a relationship coach.

At the end of September, my girlfriend went back to her ex. She hid it from me for three months, though I had already sensed something was wrong long before she said it out loud. Most of October is a blur: I couldn’t focus on work, barely ate, and didn’t want to see anyone.

Looking for help online didn’t work at first. Most of what I found was vague motivation or endless comment threads that only kept me spiraling. What actually started to shift things was learning the basic physiology behind heartbreak — why it feels like physical withdrawal — and pairing that with strict no-contact boundaries, grounding exercises, movement, and small daily actions I could finish even on bad days.

I assumed it would take far longer to get my footing back. The sadness didn’t vanish overnight; it still shows up now and then. But it stopped running my life. I learned to live alongside it, and to use it as fuel.

In early November I built Breakup Triage to be the protocol I wished I had on day one: grounded in behavioral psychology instead of empty clichés, paced one day at a time, so the first thing you take back is your routine, and then yourself.

— Kyle, indie developer and creator of Breakup Triage

08Questions

Will this make the breakup hurt less?

No. Grief follows its own timeline, and this protocol can’t make it disappear. It offers a little structure and practical support while you move through it.

I still want my ex back. Should I still do this?

Yes, and do it first. Whatever you decide later — whether to walk away for good or navigate future conversations — you need to decide from a grounded, clear, and self-respecting place, not from panic and withdrawal. When you aren’t running on craving, you take back your own power.

What if I miss a few days?

Nothing expires, and you can pick up where you left off. There are no streaks to maintain or days to make up.

Do I have to talk to anyone?

No. No calls, no live sessions, no forums. Nobody watches you use it.

Is this therapy?

No. It is an educational protocol. It doesn’t diagnose or treat anything, and it doesn’t replace a therapist, a doctor, or a crisis line.

09If you need help right now

Breakup Triage is educational and is not a crisis service. If you are in immediate danger, thinking about harming yourself, or unable to keep yourself safe, please reach out now. These lines are free and confidential.

  • UK — call 116 123 (Samaritans)
  • China — call 12356
